You start turning down business that fits your appetite because there is no time to write it. 

Submissions that used to go out the same day now take two or three.

Renewals get less attention because new business takes priority and retention can slip without anyone deciding it should.

Your best producer spends the morning on data entry instead of calls.

Errors creep into files that used to be clean. The team is not getting careless, everyone is simply moving faster than the process allows.

Each of these on its own is manageable but together they mean your brokerage has hit its submission ceiling.

The fix is to change how the work gets done.

## How to calculate the true cost of manual submission processing

The actual cost has three parts:

The first is new business intake. This covers pulling data from an application, checking it against loss runs, filling out ACORD forms and building a submission file. Time this step for your own team rather than estimating it, since it varies by line of business and by how complete the incoming data already is.

The second is renewals. Renewals get treated quicker than new business, but they still need updated loss runs, a fresh look at exposure changes and a rebuilt file if anything shifted. The time saved against new business intake is often smaller than it looks from the outside.

The third is post-submission work. This includes chasing carriers for status, re-sending documents in a different format because the first one did not work and answering underwriter questions that a cleaner file would have avoided. This part is easy to miss until you track it, and it can add up to as much time as the intake itself.

Multiply your own hours per submission by your submission volume, then by what an hour of producer time is worth to your brokerage. That total, not the time to type up one file, is the number to compare against any hire or any tool.

## Hiring an assistant for submissions: what it costs and why the backlog returns

The first instinct when volume overpowers capacity is to hire. An assistant or junior producer costs more than just the salary line suggests, since training time and ramp-up both add to what the role costs before it becomes fully productive.

Even after that hire is ramped up, the fix can be temporary.

If your brokerage keeps writing new business at the same pace, volume can grow to fill whatever capacity you add. The backlog that justified the hire can return and the brokerage is left choosing between hiring again or accepting the same ceiling as before.

Amit Chabra, InEvo's founder and CEO often says that "A hire adds capacity for a period of time but it does not change how much time each submission takes."

## What submission software can automate, and what still needs a producer

Software is good at the parts of submission work that are mechanical and repetitive. 

Retyping data from a PDF or an email into a carrier portal is one. Pulling loss run numbers and checking them against the current schedule is another. Formatting a submission file the same way every time, instead of however the last person who touched it left it, is a third. Flagging a missing field before it becomes a kickback is a fourth.

Software is not good at the parts of the job that require judgment and a relationship.

An appetite call on a borderline risk still needs a producer who knows the carrier's current mood. A conversation with an underwriter to explain an unusual exposure still needs a person on the phone. Client trust is built over years, not in a single submission and it cannot be automated. Software can take the retyping off a producer's desk. That frees up time for the calls and relationships that actually win and keep business.

## Hire or software: a five-minute test for your brokerage

Ask three questions before you decide.

First, where is the time actually going? If most of it is retyping data, checking loss runs and formatting files, that points to software. If most of it is appetite calls, carrier conversations and client relationships, no software will fix that, and a hire is the right answer.

Second, is the volume increase permanent or a temporary spike? A hire fits a permanent shift in headcount needs. A tool fits better when the bottleneck is the process itself, since it scales with volume instead of adding a fixed cost that stays whether volume is high or low that month.

Third, run the math on both. Take the true cost you calculated earlier, and price out a hire against pricing out a tool over the same twelve months. Compare your own two totals and let those numbers decide, rather than an assumption about which option is generally cheaper.

If the answer after those three questions is still unclear, that can be a sign the real bottleneck is not submissions at all. It is worth looking one level up before spending on either option.
